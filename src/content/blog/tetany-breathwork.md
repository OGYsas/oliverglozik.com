Why do my hands tingle and cramp during breathwork? **It's called tetany, and it happens because fast, deep breathing lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood, which makes your nerves fire more easily.**

It usually starts as tingling in the fingers, lips or face. If you keep breathing hard, the hands can stiffen and curl into what breathwork people call **"lobster claws"**. It looks dramatic, but **it passes within minutes once you slow your breathing down.**

In my conscious connected breathwork sessions it's very normal. **When I see someone with lobster claws, I think: okay, they're really in it.** In this post I explain why it happens, what to do when it does, and how I handle it as a facilitator.

## Tetany in breathwork at a glance

- **What it is:** tingling, stiffness or cramping, mostly in the hands, face and jaw, sometimes the feet.
- **Why it happens:** breathing faster and deeper than your body needs blows off carbon dioxide. Your blood becomes slightly alkaline, less calcium is free to steady your nerves, and they start firing on their own.
- **Is it dangerous?** For healthy people, no. It's uncomfortable and it goes away when you breathe normally.
- **What to do:** you can often keep breathing through it, especially in a session of around 30 minutes of active breathing. If it becomes too strong, slow down, soften the exhale and open your hands. Don't breathe into a paper bag.
- **Will it happen every time?** In my groups it's strongest in the first few sessions and usually eases after that.

## Why do my hands tingle and cramp during breathwork?

**Because you're breathing out carbon dioxide faster than your body makes it.** Here's the chain, step by step:

1. **Fast, deep breathing blows off carbon dioxide (CO₂).** Your body only makes so much of it. Active breathwork removes more than it produces.
2. **Your blood becomes slightly more alkaline.** Doctors call this *respiratory alkalosis*. It's the same thing that happens when someone hyperventilates during a panic attack.
3. **Less calcium is free in your blood.** In more alkaline blood, more calcium sticks to proteins, so less of it is available to the nerves. Your total calcium is normal; it's the free calcium that drops ([MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/endocrine-and-metabolic-disorders/acid-base-regulation-and-disorders/respiratory-alkalosis)).
4. **Your nerves become jumpy.** Calcium helps keep nerve cells calm. With less of it, the nerves in your hands and face start firing by themselves. You feel that as tingling, and when it spreads to the muscles, as cramping.

**It's your arms, not your brain.** In a classic 1991 study, researchers had six healthy volunteers hyperventilate on purpose and recorded their nerves. The tingling started once the carbon dioxide in their lungs had dropped by a clear amount, and the recordings showed nerves in the arm firing spontaneously. The cause was in the nerves of the limbs, not the brain ([Macefield & Burke, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/114.1.527)).

That's also why it's mainly the hands and face. Those nerves react first.

## What lobster claws look and feel like

**The mild version is tingling. Lobster claws are the strong version.** Most people move somewhere along this line:

- **Tingling or pins and needles** in the fingers, around the mouth, in the face.
- **Tension in the body.** A tight jaw is very common, sometimes stiffness in the arms or feet.
- **Lobster claws.** The wrists bend, the fingers stiffen and pull together, the thumb folds in. In medicine this is called *carpopedal spasm*. It can feel hard to open your hands.

When it first happens, most people think: what's happening here? That's normal. **The cramp is not a sign that something is going wrong.** It's a sign that you're breathing much more than usual, which is exactly what active breathwork asks of you.

## My first time

**The first time my hands cramped, it actually felt great.** I wasn't in my head any more. I was just in the rhythm of my body, my breath and the music, and it all came together.

Of course there was a moment of "okay, what's happening here?". But I'd been told beforehand that this was normal, so I wasn't worried. **That's why I always tell people before a session.** If you don't know it can happen, it's easy to panic: what's going on, this is too much. If you do know, you can stay with it.

## Is tetany during breathwork dangerous?

**For healthy people it isn't dangerous. It's uncomfortable, and it goes away on its own when your breathing returns to normal**, usually within minutes, as your carbon dioxide level comes back up.

**How common is it? In my sessions, roughly 30 to 50% of people get some tetany**, more often in their first sessions. Other breathwork facilitators quote similar numbers. The honest gap: **I found no study that has measured it**, so these figures come from experience in the room, not research. A registered breathwork trial lists tetany among the side effects reported anecdotally, together with dizziness, nausea, temperature changes and strong emotions ([NCT07129278](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/78/NCT07129278/ICF_000.pdf)). The biggest review of breathwork trials found it helps with stress, but most trials don't track side effects like this closely ([Fincham et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)).

Light-headedness and dizziness come from the same low carbon dioxide, through the blood vessels in the brain rather than the nerves in your hands. If you feel dizzy, the advice is the same: slow down.

## What to do when your hands cramp during breathwork

**You don't always have to stop. It depends on the session you're in.** In a session where the active breathing lasts around 30 minutes, like mine, you can usually keep breathing through the tingling and even some cramping. It eases again once the active part ends and your breathing settles.

**If it becomes too strong or overwhelming, slowing your breath down is what makes it pass.** These steps help:

1. **Slow the breath a little.** You don't have to stop completely. Just bring the pace and the force down.
2. **Let the exhale fall out.** Don't push the air out. A relaxed, longer exhale lets carbon dioxide build back up.
3. **Breathe through your nose for a few breaths** if you've been breathing through the mouth. It naturally slows you down.
4. **Open your hands gently** and shake them out a little. Don't force clenched fingers open.
5. **Relax your jaw.** Let the mouth go soft.
6. **Wait.** It eases within a few minutes. When it's gone, you can pick the breath up again at a gentler pace.

**Don't breathe into a paper bag.** It's the old remedy for hyperventilation, but it also lowers the oxygen you breathe in. In emergency medicine it was linked to deaths in people whose "hyperventilation" was really a lack of oxygen or a heart problem ([Callaham, 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2499228/)). Slowing your breath does the same job safely.

## Should you breathe through it or slow down?

**There are two ways of looking at tetany, and I hold both.**

The first is the physiology I described above: less carbon dioxide, less free calcium, jumpy nerves.

The second is more spiritual. **Tension is stored in the body, and when you breathe that deeply and strongly, the breath goes into the places where tension is held and lets it release.** Many breathwork schools see it this way, and some tell people to keep breathing through the cramps until they release. That's a belief from practice, not something the research has tested, but it matches what a lot of breathers feel.

What I tell people is this: **you are your own guide when you're breathing.** In a way, you're your own therapist. Listen to yourself and find the rhythm that's good for you. Breathe through the tingling if it feels okay. **If it becomes overwhelming, slow down a little.**

## How I handle tetany as a facilitator

**When someone moves into strong spasms and starts over-pushing, that's when I come closer and tell them to slow their breath down a little.** That's my line. Tingling and some tension are part of the process. When the cramps get strong and the person is forcing it, I'm not comfortable any more and I prefer to bring it down.

The length of the session matters too. In my sessions, the active breathing lasts **25 to 40 minutes**. At that length it usually doesn't get extreme. Some styles have people breathing for two or three hours, and that looks very different. I feel comfortable with my length.

## Does tetany get less with practice?

**In my experience, yes.** In my classes with regulars, people who are just starting feel a lot more tingling and tension, especially in their **first two to five sessions**. Sometimes they don't even reach the emotions underneath yet, because the body is busy with all the sensations. Not everyone, but many. **After about three to five sessions, the tingling and tension usually go down.**

It changed for me too. When I started, I breathed much harder, because I wanted to go deeper. Now I keep the active part of the breath, but **I don't over-push myself**, and that feels nurturing and soothing. I've pushed myself in so many areas of my life, breathwork included, and now I'm finding a deeper balance.

**Breathwork is a practice. You don't need to release all your pent-up emotions in one session.** It's good to come back to it. Over time you get to know your body, its limits and what level you're comfortable with.

## When it's not just breathwork tetany

**See a doctor if the tingling or cramping:**

- happens when you haven't been breathing fast,
- doesn't go away after you've been breathing normally for a while,
- is only on one side of your body,
- or comes with chest pain, fainting, a racing heart or real shortness of breath.

Tingling and muscle spasms can also come from low calcium or magnesium, hormone problems or nerve conditions. If you have epilepsy, a heart condition, are pregnant or have a serious mental health condition, talk to your doctor before doing active breathwork. Breathwork can support you, but it doesn't replace medical care.

## Questions about tetany in breathwork

### What is tetany in breathwork?
**Tingling, stiffness and cramping, mainly in the hands and face, caused by breathing fast and deep.** The low carbon dioxide makes your nerves fire on their own.

### Why are they called lobster claws?
Because the hands **stiffen and curl, with the fingers pressed together**, a bit like a claw. The medical name is carpopedal spasm.

### Does tetany mean I did the breathwork wrong?
**No.** It means you breathed much more than your body needed, which is what active breathwork does. If it gets too strong, slow down a little.

### How long does tetany last?
**Usually a few minutes after you slow down.** If it lasts much longer than that, or happens without fast breathing, see a doctor.

### Do you get tetany in Wim Hof breathing?
**You can.** The fast breathing rounds lower carbon dioxide in the same way, so tingling is common. The same rule applies: if it gets too much, slow down.

### Will I get lobster claws every time?
**Probably not.** In my groups it's strongest in the first few sessions and usually eases after three to five. For more on the different styles, read [what breathwork is and the main types](/blog/what-is-breathwork/).
